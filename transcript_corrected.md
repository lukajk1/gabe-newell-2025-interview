**[00:43] Interviewer:** It's an honor meeting you, Mr. Gabe. The first question I wanted to start with: why did you choose to become an engineer, and what was your journey?

**[00:57] Gabe:** I started programming in high school. At the time, programming wasn't really a career path. There were probably only a couple of thousand programmers in the United States, and they all worked on mainframe accounting software, primarily. Maybe there were some people doing stuff at NASA, but it wasn't like people said, "Oh, you know, there's this huge industry where software gets developed." And video games didn't even exist. I was 10 before the first video game came out, called Pong. So when I was a kid, I thought I was going to be a doctor, and programming was what I did when I should have been doing something else, right? It wasn't a career path; there were no classes in it.

My first programmable device was a calculator, a TI calculator. Through the high school that I was at, we were able to go to the University of California at Davis and access the minicomputer they had there. So we would do what was called batch programming, where you create a punch card, run the punch card to the punch card reader, and then run over to the line printer and see what your output was. The very first real video game I played was a game called Trek, which printed out the Enterprise as an E, the Romulans as an R, the Klingons as a K, and a star was an asterisk. Then you used the same random number seed at the beginning of each of your batch jobs so you could just do the next move. So our frames per second at that point were more like minutes per frame; it usually took about 15 minutes to do a move. So, once again, this was just a complete distraction and a waste of time versus what I was supposed to be doing.

I went to Harvard and, you know, continued to take programming courses, and once again it still didn't occur to me that programming was a career path. I went out to visit my brother for Thanksgiving, and he had just started a job at Microsoft. At the time, Microsoft wasn't the largest software developer in the world. It wasn't even the largest software developer in the US. It wasn't the largest software developer in Washington State. It was the third-largest software developer in the suburbs of Washington at the time. So there was no notion of it having any of the reputation that it would have, say, 10 years later.

So I was visiting my brother, and I thought he and I were going to just hang out, but he was working on a C compiler, and that's what he did all his time. So I just hung out with him at work. And Steve Ballmer got mad because I was distracting Dan from doing his job, and said, "If you're going to be hanging out here, why don't you do something useful?" So I said, "Fine, I'll do a little work," and decided I'd take the quarter off and just work at Microsoft. But I kept saying, "But I have to go back and finish my degree." And 13 years later I still [had to go back / hadn't gone back?] and finish my degree.

So that's where I learned the profession of being a developer. And it was interesting how much faster I was learning by working at Microsoft. At the time it was the best place in the world to learn how to be a programmer. It was a company that really prided itself on its ability to produce code faster and with higher quality, and to solve programming problems that nobody else was solving, and it had lots of great people to learn from. It was pretty obvious when I got there that, in terms of doing interesting work that was going to be valuable to, at the time, hundreds of thousands of people, being at Microsoft was way better than going back and continuing my education at the university.

I was there for 13 years and then decided to start Valve. Valve obviously makes video games, makes Steam, and makes hardware devices. I've also been involved in starting an educational research company called Foundry10, a neuroscience research company called Starfish, and a marine research organization called Inkfish.

**[05:56] Interviewer:** After joining Microsoft, at the moment when you understood that the gaming industry was developing fast, how did you decide on creating games and starting [Steam? / the team?]

**[06:13] Gabe:** When I was working on Windows, people would always explain to us why you didn't need graphical user interfaces, and you didn't need standard methods of [accelerating? / accessing?] graphics accelerators. So everybody would create their own sort of interface to the hardware, and they were all over the place. Some of them could only take advantage of a narrow set of aspects within the hardware; some of them were just unbelievably slow, and so on. We were trying to explain why device-independent graphics was a useful way of improving their applications, but everybody kept explaining to us that that couldn't possibly be the right solution, right? That everybody needed to develop their own method of taking advantage of graphics accelerators.

And the people who were most resistant to it were the PC games category. So we'd look at it and say, "Okay, well, maybe that's true." Then we'd look at how they were actually using the device, and it would be obvious that they were completely underutilizing the capabilities of the devices. They had one person working on it, and they weren't necessarily very good. But they were sort of under the illusion that they were going to lose a bunch of performance or capabilities by moving to Windows.

So there were two things I was doing. One, there was this whole phenomenon where id was out-distributing Microsoft with Doom, right? They had a completely alternate model of how to reach customers and engage with customers that I thought was super interesting, to the point that there were more people using Doom than were using Windows when there was a big survey done. It was also considered to be the most graphically advanced game. You couldn't see anything like Doom that would run on the consoles or in arcades. So I contacted the people at id and said, "Hey, we'll port Doom onto Windows and just give it back to you." By taking what was considered to be the most demanding video game of the time and getting it running under Windows, rather than having these debates, we could just show people: look, here's Doom, you know what it is, and here it is running under Windows. It's proof that you can have very high-performance video games. And that's when I got to know the guys at id.

So that combination presented a bunch of evidence that maybe the way of thinking about how to have customer relationships in the age of interconnectivity was wrong. The way Microsoft thought about these things, in terms of distribution and sales force and resellers and all of that, was missing the opportunity represented by the advent of networking. And I was thinking about the kind of company you would design as a result of that change in the relative value of different line-of-business functions. Sales is worth a lot less relative to product development. It used to be, in the 1920s, that the products didn't really matter; it was all about your ability to distribute those products. And then maybe in the 1950s it was like this, but in the 1990s it was going to be like this. If that's true, then you want to redesign your businesses to take advantage of that relative transition.

So I enjoyed playing video games and used that as an excuse to say, "Oh, I can go design a video game company," and I had very strong opinions about what the opportunities were to build more interesting video games. Half-Life was sort of an expression of those concepts. I was willing to put my money where my mouth was on the design of the game and on the design of the company. And that's when we started Valve, and that's where the ideas for Half-Life came from: really in response to looking at the opportunities to build what I thought was going to be a better approach to game design. And if I was wrong, I'd have to go back to Microsoft.

**[11:12] Interviewer:** In 2025, Mr. Gabe, for people in their 20s, internationals, who want to achieve success in their life, what can you recommend? What kind of advice can you give them?

**[11:29] Gabe:** Well, I think at various points there have been significant technology transitions. There was pre-computer and then post-computer; there was pre-internet and then post-internet. I think it's incredibly obvious that machine learning systems, AI systems, are going to profoundly impact pretty much every single business. So if I had to point to a technology transition to get in front of, it's to figure out how to use AI to do anything better. If you want to be an accountant, learn AI. If you're going to be an attorney, learn AI. If you're going to be a programmer, make sure that you know how to use the tools and are comfortable with those programming concepts. And that all seems super obvious.

There are a lot of people who were incredibly successful simply because they were the first person in finance at their company to learn how to use Lotus 1-2-3. Everybody else was still doing accounting in traditional ways. You would look like a super genius just because you knew how to use a desktop computer and a spreadsheet. Similarly, around the internet there were the companies and individuals who saw the opportunity and took advantage of it to create more value, either as an individual or as a corporation, and they zoomed ahead of everybody else. So essentially, AI is going to be a cheat code for people who want to take advantage of it.

And then put yourself into a situation where you can iterate in the context of feedback from a user base, and they'll teach you how to get better at what you do much faster than almost any other mechanism that I'm familiar with. So build something, whether it's a game or a website or a piece of software or some service, and iterate on it: try to perturb the system in a way where you make a predictive statement about how the customers' behaviors will change, then go find out if you were right, and then use that updated information to improve your model of how you create value for those customers, and go from there. If you can do that while capitalizing on the new capabilities that AI gives you, then I think you're off to the races and you're very, very well situated. There's going to be a gigantic shortage of people across all industries who understand how to take advantage of it.

I'm serious: everything is going to be impacted. If you're on a boat and you're a captain, and you can figure out how to get your bridge systems to take advantage of machine learning, it's going to make you ten times as valuable as captains who don't understand that. Or if you're a bridge system developer, like if you're Kongsberg and you have closed proprietary systems (I'm not saying they do, but if you do), then you're going to get crushed by somebody being able to do stuff on their iPhone that your multi-billion-dollar bridge system can't do, simply because it doesn't have an easy way to take advantage of the capabilities in the open systems. Does that make sense?

**[14:43] Interviewer:** Yeah. As a multi-billionaire, a person who has achieved high success in life, who is a legend in the gaming industry, what would you say separates people who succeed from those who don't?

**[15:06] Gabe:** There's no one-size-fits-all thing. Each person has to kind of figure that out on their own. It's an inconvenient truth that there are no simple slogans, no simple things that you can do. Everything that is a good idea for one person at a certain point in time is a terrible idea for somebody else. So, I mean, the tactics I would suggest are: make sure that you have people around you that you can trust, that you can have transparent communications with, and just engage with them to think about how you can get better. But it's never like "follow your passion." That sounds like a great idea, but lots of people follow their passions right into a crater. And then there's a selection bias, or survival bias, where people who are successful go back and say, "It's because I wear a yellow shirt. Everybody should wear a yellow shirt," and, you know, correlation is not causation and stuff like that.

So the tactic I would strongly suggest is: think about the people that you work with and collaborate with, and make sure they're the best people you can find, because they'll help pull you through whatever developmental challenges you might have, and each person's challenges are going to be different. Some people have motivational challenges, some people have training or knowledge gaps, some people haven't found which field they're really going to contribute the most in. And so the advice for each one of those people is going to be different and contingent on where you're coming from. So I tend to view it as: there's never a one-size-fits-all approach to achieving those kinds of things. It's going to be idiosyncratic and [individual?], and the key is to have the people around you who will help you make that progress. It's just a lot easier when you have somebody who can slap you upside the head and say, "You're being stupid, stop it," or "You really need to take a risk here," or things like that, giving you good advice. But that's really tactical, right? There are lots of examples of people who haven't had that sort of [...] social graph around them to help them, but it certainly makes it easier. It simplifies the challenge; it doesn't define the challenge that people are going to be facing.

**[19:29] Interviewer:** People who don't have access to the Bay Area and big tech corporations, but who want to learn a lot and study, who don't have access to the big companies and big universities: what can you say to them? Should they come to the US, or do they not need to, and can they do it in their own countries? [How does the region affect this?]

**[20:05] Gabe:** So I think there used to be a lot of value to certain kinds of geographic co-location. I think that value has gone down enormously, both for technical reasons and because of everybody having to retrain. So, you know, I live on a boat nowadays, right? I haven't been back to Bellevue in a couple of years, and I'm perfectly capable of working and engaging with a lot of people on a wide range of projects. And I think that's the key thing, whether you're in the CIS region or Silicon Valley or wherever: find your team. Don't assume that your team is somehow located in Silicon Valley or that you have to go to the Bay Area in order to get something done. Focus on delivering value to people right now, rather than viewing your geographic location as some fundamental impediment, right?

Like I said, I'm not located with everybody else at Valve right now, and I don't think about that as the biggest… There are many problems that I have, and where I'm physically located is pretty low on the list, right? I'm spending all my time trying to think through the implications of machine learning on video game design and video game services. I'm not thinking about how this would be easier if I was in a physically different location. The opportunities are there regardless of where you are in the world.

Thirty years ago, it was much more of a problem. Even five years ago, it was more of a problem. One of the problems you would have had before the COVID pandemic kicked off was that a lot of people just weren't used to working with people remotely. But that forced everybody to retrain around remote work, and so even the hysteresis around that kind of organizational transition is gone. There are lots of teams working in a highly distributed fashion: one person is from Brazil, one person is from Greece, one person is from Korea, and they're perfectly capable of creating value. The much harder problem is figuring out what it is that you're going to work on that is useful for people. Just dig in, make a tool for the other students around you, and then iterate on it for five or six generations to see what your users are going to teach you. That's a way more interesting way to frame the challenge, I think, than trying to figure out which geographic location you need to be in.

**[23:08] Interviewer:** In 2025, you recommend that people use AI heavily. I wanted to ask: do we need to learn the technical side, or do we need to learn how to use AI, the tools we have right now, and use them at a maximum level?

**[23:33] Gabe:** I think it's both. The more you understand what underlies these current tools, the more effective you are at taking advantage of them. But I think we'll be in this funny situation where people who don't know how to program, who use AI to scaffold their programming abilities, will become more effective developers of value than people who have been programming for a decade. So even if you're just a pure tool user, you're going to find that the gains from utilizing those tools are very, very high. But I think your ability to use those tools will continue to improve the more you understand the underlying methods and mindsets of people developing machine learning systems. So I think it's both, and I think they're highly, highly complementary.

If you're a filmmaker and you don't understand how to use AI, you're really going to struggle in the not-too-distant future. But if you're an AI tool user, you're going to be even better if you think to yourself, "Oh, I could spin up a simple model to solve a very narrow problem that I have, and I understand its strengths and limitations, and why I can get away with not using a foundation model to solve the technical problem, because I understand the broadness of the domain and the utility of narrow modeling." Those people are going to be super useful, and they're going to be kind of off to the races. I'm going to pick filmmaking because I think AI is going to be ten times as significant as the impact CGI had on filmmaking, in terms of the value of being accomplished with those technologies and how it would affect your career trajectory in the filmmaking industry. Multiply that by an order of magnitude. I'm just picking a random industry in filmmaking.

And similarly, if you're a tool user, it will help you understand the capabilities, when a lot of other people will be reading articles in Variety to try to understand what its impact is going to be on the industry, whereas some 19-year-old kid in his or her bedroom will be like, "Oh, you guys really don't understand this. I actually use this tool, and unless you're a regular user of it and have seen how it's evolving and have a sense of where it's going, you're going to completely miss the opportunities to take advantage of it." And that same person in their bedroom, the more they try to write a little bit of code or dig into the research papers on the topic, the more they're going to find they're accelerating that process even further.

**[26:28] Interviewer:** Yeah. I wanted to [ask] from the business perspective, Mr. Gabe. People who are studying business degrees, who want to open businesses or create something useful for people: what advice can you give them before starting a business or going into the business field, or finishing business degrees?

**[26:51] Gabe:** So, people don't buy businesses, right? They buy value that you create for them. You shouldn't ever think of yourself as "I'm going into a business." You should think, "This is the value that I'm going to create for customers." That's the thing you need to be really focused on: what is the value that you're creating for customers? Why is the value you're creating for those customers different from the value that other people are offering them, and how can you increase that value? So focusing on your customers and how to be valuable to them, to the exclusion of almost everything else, is a really good approach, right?

I see a lot of people go into situations thinking that what they need is a pitch document for VCs to raise capital. That's a deeply distracted beginning to an organization. If you're creating value for people, the capital will come your way, and probably at a lower cost than it would otherwise. Having a big bunch of capital and then saying, "Oh, I guess, given all those lies we told in our pitch deck, now we have to go hire a whole bunch of people to be on this trajectory": I think that's a great way of destroying a bunch of money and wasting a bunch of people's time. So I think the key is to ignore all of the distractions around it and just focus on how we make our customers happier. If you listen to your customers and focus on them, it's ridiculously easier to build a business. The focus should always be on your customers, on your partners, and on your employees, and then everything else will fall into place over time.

Almost all the discussions about branding that I hear nowadays are like, "Oh my god, you are so deeply distracted and confused about what you're trying to do here." Most of what goes on in the business press targeting small companies is the exact opposite of what people [need; it puts?] the spotlight in the wrong place. It's: who are your customers, what do they care about, why would they care about this more than that? Those are the questions people should be asking themselves. And until you can sort of read your customers' minds, and are continuously willing to be surprised by your customers, all the other stuff is really a distraction.

**[29:39] Interviewer:** What is the difference between billionaires and regular people?

**[29:44] Gabe:** In my case, it was luck, and it was being surrounded by really great people, right? I'm not going to speak broadly; I haven't ever bothered to study the subject of billionaires. Whenever I read anything about it, the analysis is really unsound. There's this thing called survivor bias, where if you study the people who are in a certain category, you don't realize that just because they share a characteristic, there may be no causality to it at all. So in my case, I was very lucky, in multiple dimensions. I've had the good fortune, at the beginning of my career, to work with people like Neil Konzen and Doug Klunder and Jeff Harbers, and then to start companies with people like Yahn and Jay and Scott, and to continue to be lucky enough to be around people like them, and to have the roll of the dice continue to go my way. I think that's how I've ended up where I am. It'd be great to say I'm just this absolutely wonderful person and that I earned all of this and that it expresses my awesomeness, but there was an awful lot of luck that went into it.

**[31:16] Interviewer:** What is the daily routine of Mr. Gabe?

I live on a boat, so I just hang out with everybody on the boat, then I work. I work seven days a week. I'm working from my bedroom, as you can tell. I like working; it's fun. To me it doesn't feel like work. The kinds of things that I get to do every day are super awesome. I've said it before, but when you retire, you want to stop doing your horrible job and go do what is most fun and entertaining. In that sense, I've been retired for a long time, right? But it's not like, "Oh my god, I'm up late at night slaving away on stuff." So I have a pretty boring life: I scuba dive and I work, and that's pretty darn cool. I love working. Like I said, it doesn't feel like work most of the time.
